Carlos Arleo

I’m an architect who studies the rules, materials and data that decide what gets built, and builds tools that make them open to challenge.

Fifteen years in practice, including research at OMA/AMO and regenerative masterplanning in Africa and the Caribbean, led me from designing buildings to asking what shapes them.

My doctoral work in critical urban theory and regenerative design follows that question through planning policy (a 652-site audit of English planning; Your Planning Voice), material supply chains (Atelier Terre, a circular-economy framework for earth construction) and public data. Where I use AI, it proposes and explicit rules check.

Current projects

Each tool pairs an AI model that proposes with explicit rules that check.

Atelier Terre

In development

An open-source platform that adapts the UKGBC circular-economy framework to compressed-earth construction in Marrakesh. A single catalogue of 41 materials and 9 block products is the source of truth, generating a web platform, IFC export, and a native Revit workflow that draws, counts, and checks every block. Developed with a producer in Marrakech.

Interactive framework Repository (AGPL-3.0)

Your Planning Voice

In use

A tool that helps community groups draft planning objections grounded in planning policy. An AI model drafts, and rule-based checks stop anything the evidence doesn’t support. It has been used on three live cases (Carrington Moss, Lapwing Green Spaces, Mowbray BESS) and is developed with the Community Planning Alliance.

yourplanningvoice.com

DNI

In testing

A tool that assesses how novel a piece of research is by comparing it with retrieved prior work. Tested on the Liu & Zhai (2026) benchmark. An earlier version received a Special Mention in the UKRI Metascience Novelty Indicators Challenge, and a calibration study with SPRU at the University of Sussex is in development.

Research on English planning

Doctoral research on the consultation stage of English planning.

652contested planning sites audited
852community submissions
65government datasets cross-referenced

My doctoral work audits 652 contested planning sites, drawn from 852 community submissions and cross-referenced against 65 government datasets. The main findings are:

Protection is uneven
Ecological protection is more common in more affluent areas, while heritage protection is more common in less affluent ones.
Farmland
Speculative infrastructure proposals concentrate on high-grade agricultural land.
Data gaps
Ecological records are thinnest in the commuter-belt fringe, and several national datasets were unavailable or restricted when I queried them.
Limits
I found no detectable difference in planning outcomes, so the work concerns the consultation stage rather than final decisions.
